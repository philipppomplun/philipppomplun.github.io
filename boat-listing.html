Making a listing scannable

On a boat marketplace, every listing opened with a wall of more than 30 data rows. Buyers had to read all of it to find the four things they actually compare first. I restructured the listing around those four facts – and explored how much detail should stay visible.

01

Everything at once, nothing first

Directly below the price, the original listing showed every specification the seller had entered – year, condition, dimensions, draught, displacement, keel type, cabins, berths, engine hours, sail areas and more. All in the same weight, in one long list.

  • No hierarchy. The year mattered as much as the size of the spinnaker – at least visually.
  • Key info scattered. Size sat in row three, engine power in row eighteen, location only far below the list.
  • Long way to the story. Location, the seller’s remarks and equipment – what makes a boat interesting – started after more than a full screen of data.
02

Approach

I sorted the data by what buyers compare first when they shortlist a boat, and what they only check once a boat is a serious option. Four facts stood out as decision-relevant at first glance: year, dimensions, location and engine power.

If the four most important facts are visible at a glance,

then buyers can judge a listing faster and contact sellers more often,

because they no longer have to read a full data sheet to decide whether a boat is relevant.

03

Design decisions

Four key facts with icons

Year, dimensions, location and engine power as a scannable block with icons and labels – right below the price.

Labels above values

Small uppercase labels make each fact readable on its own, without a second column to match.

Two levels of detail

VAR 01 keeps the full list below the key facts. VAR 02 collapses it behind “more details” – for buyers who already know they’re interested.

The story moves up

With details collapsed, location, remarks and equipment appear much earlier – the content that makes people fall for a boat.

04

Measuring success

The variants went into an A/B test against the original. The results weren’t available to me at the time of writing.

The metrics that would tell whether the restructuring worked: seller enquiries as the primary goal, plus saved listings and how many users still open the full details in VAR 02 – a direct signal of how much of the data people really need.

05

What I took away

Prioritising is the design work

No data was removed. The value of the redesign lies entirely in deciding what comes first – which made talking to the client about buyer behaviour as important as the visual design.

Progressive disclosure needs a reason to expand

Collapsing details only works if the summary is strong enough to answer the first question – and the link to “more details” is clear enough for the second.
