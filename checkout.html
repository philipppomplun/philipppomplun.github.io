Checkout entry restructuring

The first checkout step asks every buyer one question: how do you want to continue? On this furniture shop the answer was hard to find – guest checkout was buried under registration, and the main buttons looked disabled. I made every path obvious.

01

Three ways forward, none of them obvious

Before a buyer can enter an address, the checkout asks them to log in, create an account or continue as a guest. On the original page, that decision was harder than it needed to be:

  • Guest checkout was hidden under registration. It sat at the bottom of the “Register” box – not where someone who doesn’t want an account would look for it.
  • The primary actions didn’t look primary. The login button was a muted grey that reads as disabled, and all registration options were identical outline buttons with no hierarchy.
02

Hypothesis

If guest checkout is shown as a standalone option and the login options are clearly prioritised,

then more users will reach the next step and complete their purchase,

because they find the option they’re looking for much more easily.

03

Design decisions

The variant kept layout, fields and copy almost untouched. Instead, it changed how clearly each path presents itself – so any effect could be traced back to that.

Primary actions look clickable

Login and “create account” became filled buttons in the brand colour instead of grey or outline.

Guest checkout gets its own card

“Continue without account” is now a separate, equally visible decision – not the last item under registration.

Less to compare in one box

The registration card shrank from three stacked options to one primary and one secondary action.

Clear hierarchy per card

Every card has exactly one main action, so users can scan by intent: log in, sign up or just buy.

04

Results: a clear win

The variant ran as an A/B test against the original. Both primary goals moved significantly:

  • Sales +6.92% – significant
  • Logins +8.47% – significant
  • Registrations +13.70% and guest orders +3.76% – positive, but not significant

Projected over a year, the uplift in sales amounts to a six-figure additional revenue for the retailer.

05

What I took away

Visual weight is a promise

Users read a grey button as “not available yet”. Giving the main actions the visual weight they deserve was the cheapest change on the page – and likely the most effective one.

Different intents need different places

“I want an account” and “I just want to buy” are separate decisions. Putting them in separate containers lets each user find their path without reading everything else first.

Focused changes give clear answers

Unlike a full redesign, this test changed only the hierarchy of existing options – which made the result easy to attribute and easy to roll out.
